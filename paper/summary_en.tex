\begin{center}
{\Large\bfseries Feeling Understood Is Not Feeling Ownership}\\[3pt]
{\normalsize Survey, interaction-log, and microsimulation evidence from CoAuthor \textemdash{} two-page summary}\\[2pt]
{\small Hyoyeon (Elina) Lee \quad 2026-10-09 \quad \url{https://github.com/yeona-lee-researcher/coauthor-ownership-analysis}}
\end{center}

\head{(1) Motivation}
What users value when writing with AI may be not only the quality of the text but whether it still carries \emph{their} intent.
Evaluating AI collaboration by usage or output quality alone misses the experience of being helped while remaining the author.
I asked whether feeling understood by an AI writing assistant goes together with feeling that the resulting text is one's own,
and separated stable differences between writers from variation across the same writer's sessions, since CoAuthor writers completed up to 89 sessions each.

\head{(2) Hypothesis}
\textbf{H1 (primary):} in sessions where a writer felt \emph{more understood than usual}, ownership is higher, after accounting for the human share of text, prompt, and model setting.
H2: feeling understood goes with ideation help. H3: the ideation--ownership link is stronger when understanding is higher.
Directions were fixed before the first model run (not preregistered). Log analyses and the simulation were added after the survey results and are exploratory.

\head{(3) Approach}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Survey models.} Linked metadata and surveys (1,407 sessions with at least one AI request; 61 writers). Split each rating into a writer mean and a within-writer deviation; ordinal GEE with writer-clustered errors; ten sensitivity analyses and a writer bootstrap.
  \item \textbf{Log replay.} Replayed all 1,447 keystroke logs, tagging every character as prompt, human, or AI. This reproduces the metadata's human share within 1 pp in 99.9\% of sessions and its selection counts in 100\%. Sessions were segmented into writing, pause ($\ge$10\,s), and request episodes (accepted / rejected / unanswered).
  \item \textbf{Microsimulation.} A semi-Markov model of these episodes with writer-level shrinkage, session-level variation, and writer stopping. Validated on unseen writers (5-fold by writer) and on the later half of each frequent writer's sessions; tuning used training data only.
  \item \textbf{Qualitative.} Contrastive sampling of 23 comments (16 writers) where understanding and ownership agree or diverge; preliminary AI-assisted codes grouped into five themes. Human review is pending.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Surveys.} No sensitivity analysis gave a positive within-writer understanding--ownership link. The between-writer link (OR 2.36) weakened to 1.37 when each writer was weighted equally.
  \item \textbf{Logs.} 6.8\% of requests were never answered. Requests came a median 0.4\,s after typing; only 2.1\% followed $\ge$10\,s of inactivity.
  \item \textbf{Simulation.} Knowing the \emph{order} of actions predicts unseen writers far better than knowing only their frequencies (log-likelihood per event $-1.27 \rightarrow -0.80$). Writer-specific parameters predicted the same writer's later sessions better than a population model: +0.068 nats/event [0.041, 0.106], 35 of 39 writers. This is a log-score gain, not an accuracy percentage.
  \item \textbf{Qualitative.} Help and ownership diverged in both directions. One writer rated understanding and ideation 7 but ownership 2, describing themselves as ``closer to an editor than a writer.'' Another rated understanding 3 but ownership 7. Low understanding ratings often accompanied service complaints (no suggestions, latency), which the log result above confirms.
\end{itemize}

Coverage shows how realistically the model anticipates the range of behavior; it is not accuracy.
Error in predicting human share also fell from 15.6 to 10.7 pp. Coverage stays below .90, so much session-to-session variation remains unexplained.
Writer clustering was high (ICC .56--.84). An in-sample check reproduced most of it in creative writing, less in argumentative writing,
and not for duration: overall session length was predicted well, but not which writers consistently write long or short sessions.

\head{(5) Findings}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Feeling understood is not feeling ownership.} Within a writer, understanding tracked ideation help, not ownership; ownership tracked how much the writer typed.
  \item \textbf{``Understanding'' ratings partly measure availability.} They fell when the system failed to respond, so a single satisfaction-style score mixes model quality, service availability, and ideation support.
  \item \textbf{AI is called in the flow of writing, not after a stall.} Accepting a suggestion was followed by more human writing 86--87\% of the time: acceptance is part of an ongoing process, not its end.
  \item \textbf{Recurring individual styles over a shared rhythm.} A writer's past collaboration predicts their future collaboration, while the same writer still varies from session to session. These are behavioral regularities in this task, not measures of personality or motivation.
  \item \textbf{Failures change the experience more visibly than the text.} In a model-implied scenario (not causal) that answers the never-answered requests, accepted suggestions rise by 0.53 per session and the human share of inserted characters falls by 1.3 pp. More AI-inserted text is not less human direction.
\end{enumerate}

\claim{\textbf{Central claim.} CoAuthor interactions show both recurring writer-specific patterns and session-level variation.
A writer's past record improved prediction of later sessions, but this predictability is not a direct measure of personality or intrinsic motivation.
Designing human--AI collaboration should consider the shared action rhythm, each user's history, and the current session's context together,
and should evaluate ideation help, availability, and ownership separately.}

\noindent\textbf{Next question.} Beyond ``does this person usually use AI a lot?'', ask: \emph{what role does this person want in this task, and does the current form of help fit it?}
Predicting past behavior well does not mean reinforcing it is good personalization.
A follow-up experiment would ask writers for their desired role before writing and manipulate an intent-confirmation interface and time budget, testing whether role alignment explains ownership and satisfaction.

\noindent\textbf{Limits.} Single-item post-session ratings; observational and partly post hoc; human share counts inserted, not final, characters;
the simulation under-reproduces writers who rarely use AI and writer-level duration differences; qualitative codes await human review.
